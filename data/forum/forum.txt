[QUESTION]
Bayes risk of decision rule and Bayesian expected loss. 

What's the difference between these two? I only know one of the
differences is we don't have the data in the former (prior), but we know
the data in the latter (posterior). Is there any other difference? Could
you please help me distinguish between these two and frequentist risk?

[ANSWER]
The FREQUENTIST FRAMEWORK treats the parameter th as a fixed but unknown
quantity, and the expectation is constructed by averaging over the data,
typically based on sampling distributions. In this framework, the Risk
function is defined as the expected loss over the sampling distribution of
the data, and decisions are made by minimizing this risk function.

In contrast, the BAYESIAN FRAMEWORK treats the parameter th as a random
variable with a probability distribution, and expectations are constructed
by averaging over this distribution, typically using the posterior given
the observed data. The Bayesian expected loss is computed by averaging the
loss function over the posterior distribution of th, accounting for both
the data and the uncertainty about th.

You can think of BAYES RISK as a "hybrid" between the _Bayesian_ and
_Frequentist_ frameworks. The key transition from the Risk function (in the
Frequentist approach) to Bayes Risk occurs when we move from treating th as
a fixed, unknown parameter to recognizing that th is uncertain and follows
a probability distribution. In this way, Bayes Risk generalizes the
Frequentist Risk by incorporating uncertainty about th. This allows the
decision-making process to account not only for the potential outcomes of
different decisions but also for the uncertainty surrounding the true value
of th.

Thus, Bayes Risk is a "hybrid" because it combines the idea of Risk
function (a frequentist concept) with the uncertainty about the parameter
th (a Bayesian concept).

If the acf graph of our data doesn't show geometric decay and the pacf

[ANSWER]
If the true model of a process is an autoregressive (AR) model, the
autocorrelation function (ACF) graph will show geometric decay. This is
because in an AR model, the current value of the process depends only on
its previous values, and as the lag between the current value and previous
values increases, the correlation between them decreases exponentially,
leading to geometric decay in the ACF graph.

However, the ACF graph of the real data does not always have to show
geometric decay. The shape of the ACF graph can vary depending on the
nature of the data. 

In PACF graph, I suggested that in this ICA it may be beneficial to ignore

theoretical justification for doing so (and this would be fine in terms of
marking, i.e. no marks are lost if you do that). However, if this leads to
a model where the autocorrelation in the residuals is still detected using
Ljung-Box test (and also the model cannot pass the AD/KS tests), then I
would suggest taking those lags into account, either individually or all
together, until you have accounted for the autocorrelation.

[QUESTION]
Getting to posterior gamma
Can you please explain how we got to that conclusion ? Looking at the posterior distribution in  page 6 of the week 7 lecture
notes, I don't seem to see how we got that pdf to be that of a gamma (alpha
+n, beta + sum...).

[ANSWER]
We have the likelihood function as follows:

[ L(\gamma | y_1, \ldots, y_n) = \prod_{t=1}^{n} \gamma e^{-\gamma y_t} ]
[1]

and the prior distribution for [ \gamma] [2] is [ Gamma(\alpha, \beta)]
[3]:

[ p(\gamma) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \gamma^{\alpha - 1}
e^{-\beta \gamma} ] [4]

We want to find the posterior distribution of [ \gamma ] [5] given the
data [ y_1, \ldots, y_n] [6]. Using Bayes' theorem, the posterior
distribution is proportional to the product of the prior distribution and
the likelihood function:

[ p(\gamma | y_1, \ldots, y_n) \propto L(\gamma | y_1, \ldots, y_n)
p(\gamma)\\ \propto \left( \prod_{t=1}^{n} \gamma e^{-\gamma y_t} \right)
\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \gamma^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta \gamma}\\
\propto \gamma^{n+\alpha-1} e^{-\gamma \left( \beta + \sum_{t=1}^{n} y_t
\right)} ] [7]

So, the posterior distribution is [ Gamma\left( \alpha + n, \beta +
\sum_{t=1}^{n} y_t\right) ] [8].

[ \begin{aligned} \textrm{We know that}\quad & \tilde{y} \sim N\left(\mu,

\sigma^2\right)&=\operatorname{var}\left(\mu \mid \sigma^2,

=\sigma_n^2+\sigma^2\\ \end{aligned} ] [4]

Hence, we established that

[ \tilde{y} \mid y \sim N\left(\mu_n, \sigma_n^2+\sigma^2\right) ] [5]

Recall that the (unconditional) variance of a random variable u_(t-1) with
mean 0 is E[u^2 _(t-1)], which is sigma^2_(t-1).

Also, the (unconditional) variance of a random variable u_t with mean 0 is
E[u^2 _t], which is sigma^2_t.

One crucial detail to note here is that we rely on the assumption of WEAK
STATIONARITY of a time series. Weak stationarity is a statistical property
of a time series where the mean, variance, and autocovariance do not change
over time. Therefore, the variance of u_t is the same as the variance of
u_(t-1). Thus,  sigma^2_t=sigma^2_(t-1)=sigma^2. This holds for any t.

P.S. A TIME SERIES of a random variable Y_t is a sequence of observations
of Y at different time points t (e.g. in our case we assume log-returns are
weakly stationary). Each observation in the series is a realization of the
random variable Y at a specific time point t.

[QUESTION]
What is the difference between the posterior distribution and posterior
predictive distribution?
what *exactly* are their uses cases that make them differnet?

[ANSWER]
The POSTERIOR DISTRIBUTION is obtained by applying the Bayes' theorem to
update the prior belief about th using the observed data. It provides a
description of the uncertainty about th after considering the data. 

On the other hand, the POSTERIOR PREDICTIVE DISTRIBUTION is derived using
the posterior distribution. For example, consider the derivation of
the posterior predictive distribution presented on page 41 of Lecture 4:

[ \begin{aligned} p(\tilde{y} \mid \mathbf{y}) & =\int p(\tilde{y}, \theta
\mid \mathbf{y}) d \theta \\ & =\int p(\tilde{y} \mid \theta, \mathbf{y})
p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}) d \theta \\ & =\int p(\tilde{y} \mid \theta)
p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}) d \theta \end{aligned} ] [1]

The main idea to have in mind here is that we want to make predictions or
forecasts for new data points. That is, we need a distribution of
possible outcomes of [ \tilde{y} ] [2]. Suppose we know the
distribution [ p(\tilde{y} \mid \theta) ] [3]. However, it can be noted
that this distribution depends on th, which is unknown. We can employ the
Bayesian approach in this context by treating th as a random variable, and
therefore, we can integrate it out. In order to do that, we take the
following steps:

	*
First line: We start with the joint density of [ \tilde{y} ] [2] and [
\theta ] [4] given the observed data  [ \mathbf{y} ] [5]. It is
represented as [ p(\tilde{y}, \theta \mid \mathbf{y}) ] [6].

	*
Second line: We know that [ p(\tilde{y}, \theta \mid \mathbf{y}) ]
[6] can be rewritten as the product of two conditional densities: [
p(\tilde{y} \mid \theta, \mathbf{y}) ] [7] and [ p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y})
] [8]. 

	*
Third line: We use the assumption that [ \tilde{y} ] [2] is conditionally
independent of the observed data  [ \mathbf{y} ] [5] given the parameter [
\theta ] [4]. In other words, [ \tilde{y} ] [2]  only depends on [ \theta
] [4] and is not influenced by the observed data. Therefore, we can drop
the conditioning on [ \mathbf{y} ] [5] in the conditional distribution [
p(\tilde{y} \mid \theta, \mathbf{y}) ] [7], resulting in [ p(\tilde{y} \mid
\theta) ] [9]. Therefore, the integral becomes [ \int p(\tilde{y} \mid
\theta) p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}) d \theta = p(\tilde{y} \mid \mathbf{y}) ]
[10].

Finally, we obtain the POSTERIOR PREDICTIVE DISTRIBUTION [ p(\tilde{y}
\mid \mathbf{y}) ] [11].

Would you be able to provide some insight as to how this value at risk
formula for multiperiod returns over a h-day horizon is derived?

[ANSWER]
So here we used the additive property of log-returns. That
is, if we want to find the log-return over, say, two periods, then we can
simply add log-returns in period 1 and period 2. In the formula above we
assume i.i.d. normality, so the variance of the sum of two log-returns is
simply the sum of the variances of individual log-returns (using the
independence assumption), i.e. 2s^2. If we want to find the variance of
log-returns over the next h periods, then we add individual variances h
times, which yields hs^2 (to get the standard deviation, we take the
square root).

Remember that our decision rule is [

First we multiply and divide by [ \sigma^2 ] [2] so the constant  becomes
